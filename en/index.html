In any single round, defecting earns you more whatever they do. But if you both defect, you each get 1 point instead of 3.

Why it’s a dilemma

Look at the table from your side. If they cooperate, defecting gets you 5 points instead of 3. If they defect, defecting gets you 1 instead of 0. Either way, defecting pays more in a single round.

The other player can reason the same way. When you both defect, you each get 1 point when you could have had 3. Play many rounds with the same partner, though, and a defection today can cost you tomorrow. Robert Axelrod called this the shadow of the future.

Axelrod’s tournament

In 1980 the political scientist Robert Axelrod invited game theorists to submit computer programs to play this game. Fourteen programs, plus one that moved at random, each played every other program and a copy of itself for 200 rounds.

The simplest program entered won: Tit for Tat, by Anatol Rapoport. It cooperates on the first move, then copies whatever its opponent did last. In a second tournament, 62 entrants knew that result and many tried to beat it. Tit for Tat won again.

Axelrod then simulated evolution. Each generation, programs got copies in proportion to their scores. Exploiters grew while easy targets lasted, then shrank once those targets were gone.

What winning strategies have in common

Axelrod found four habits shared by the programs that did well.

Nice

Never the first to defect.

Tit for Tat against itself cooperates from the first round to the last.

Retaliatory

Hits back right away, so exploiting it doesn’t pay.

Tit for Tat gets exploited once by Always Defect, then never again.

Forgiving

Goes back to cooperating as soon as the other player does.

One defection earns one punishment, then cooperation resumes.

Clear

Easy to read, so others learn quickly how to get along with it.

Detective tests Tit for Tat once, gets punished, and settles down.
